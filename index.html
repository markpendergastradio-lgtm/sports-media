Project 2

Sports news bulletin

UNIT 5 · YOUTHS CHOICE · RCS ZETTA

Write, record and edit your own 3-minute sports news bulletin: 5 stories, at least 3 of them with a clip. Every word of the script is yours, so don't copy from websites. The finished product is one exported audio file plus your script, uploaded.

Resources: IRN sports news clips · Script template site · Sports news bed MP3 (on Showbie) · Zetta2GO (your teacher gives you the login)

How to rank your stories

Ask three questions about each one. Is it new today, and in the most popular sport? Does it matter to my listeners, for example a big club, a local team or a major tournament? Is there a clip that brings it to life? The story that scores highest goes first. Put a lighter or local story last.

Writing for radio

  • Short sentences, one idea in each.
  • Write numbers the way you'd say them: "two-nil", not "2-0".
  • Say who is speaking before each clip plays.
  • Aim for about 30–35 seconds per story, including its clip.

Script template

  • Intro (5s): "This is [name] with the sports news on Youths Choice."
  • Stories 1–3 (35s each): headline, then a cue of 2–3 sentences, then the clip intro ("[Speaker] said..."). Log each clip as file name, IN: "[first words]", OUT: "[last words]", and length in seconds. Then one sentence to back-announce it.
  • Stories 4–5 (25s each): copy only, 3–4 sentences.
  • Outro (5s): "That's the sport on Youths Choice, I'm [name]."

Then into Zetta

Put your bulletin over the sports news bed, load it into RCS Zetta, label it in the house format and play it out. Then your teacher drops in a late story: re-rank, rewrite and play out the shaken-up version with no dead air.

Before you leave

  • 5 stories, biggest first
  • At least 3 clips from Sky, each 10–20 seconds and introduced
  • Total length 2:50–3:10
  • Exported as Y12_Surname_Bulletin_v1.wav (or .mp3)
  • Audio file and script uploaded to the class Drive folder
  • Screenshot of your edit timeline saved for your weekly log
  • Two lines in your weekly log: one thing that worked, one thing you'd change

Pass

5 stories, 3 clips, about 3 minutes, clear voice, exported and uploaded.

Merit and Distinction

Merit: stories in a sensible order with the biggest first, clips introduced and back-announced, even levels and no gaps or mistakes. Distinction: sounds like a professional bulletin: tight radio writing, confident delivery, a well-chosen mix of clips, and timing within 2:50–3:10.

Shoot Better Content: filming basics

YEAR 12 · LIGHTING · COMPOSITION · SCENE

The scenario: you are a junior content creator at a sports media channel. New creators keep sending in footage that is badly lit, badly framed or filmed in front of distracting backgrounds, and it can't be fixed in the edit. Your producer wants a filming basics guide that shows new creators, side by side, what good and bad camerawork looks like.

Film GOOD and BAD pairs for each skill

  • Lighting: direction of light, soft vs hard, exposure, avoiding silhouettes and mixed light.
  • Composition and framing: rule of thirds, headroom, look room and lead room.
  • Scene selection and backgrounds: relevant, clean backgrounds with depth; no clutter, poles or photobombers.

Every BAD example must be deliberate: show the mistake on purpose, then prove you know how to fix it. Only the target technique should change between GOOD and BAD.

Deliverables

  1. Raw clips: at least 2 GOOD/BAD pairs per skill (12+ clips), max 10 seconds each, labelled clearly (e.g. LIGHTING_BAD_01). Upload to OneDrive.
  2. Comparison edit (2–4 min): your pairs cut together side by side or before/after, with captions naming each technique and the mistake.
  3. Written evaluation on your site: for each pair, what you did, why the bad version fails and how you fixed it.

Stretch (aiming for Distinction): present it as a tutorial video, with voiceover or pieces to camera teaching each technique in your own style. Watch the Shoot Better Content tutorial series made by a former Global Academy student (link on Showbie) before you plan your shoot.

Kit and rules

  • Phone (grid on) or school cameras, booked and signed out; tripods from the kit room
  • Edit in Premiere Pro or CapCut, with readable captions
  • Lesson decks on Showbie: Composition & Framing, Lighting Basics, Scene Selection
  • Film only in agreed areas; ask before anyone appears in shot or you film logos; recce first; watch for trip hazards
  • The GOOD/BAD pairs you filmed in lessons count, so start from what you have

Pass

All three skills covered with labelled GOOD/BAD pairs, visible differences, and an evaluation that says what changed in each pair.

Merit and Distinction

Merit: clearly deliberate, well-controlled pairs where only the target technique changes, an organised captioned edit, and an evaluation explaining why each mistake damages the shot and how you fixed it. Distinction: imaginative, confident work that teaches the viewer: tutorial-style, sports-media locations and scenarios, accurate technical vocabulary and honest reflection.

Clips + edit on OneDrive (link on Showbie)Evaluation on your site

Format

Choose your podcast format

Investigative featureRadio debateAudio documentaryDeep-dive sports media explainer

Questions

What your essay can explore

  • How have rights, distribution and audiences moved from traditional TV, radio and print to streaming, social, YouTube, podcasts and club channels?
  • Is the audience fragmenting, and who holds it now?
  • How do athletes and clubs reach fans directly, and what does that mean for broadcasters and journalists?

Build it

A structure that works

ESSAY
  • Introduction (about 150 words): your answer to the question and the four areas you'll cover.
  • Main body (about 1,000 words): four sections, one per media type. Make your point, give the evidence, explain what it proves.
  • Conclusion (about 200 words): return to your argument, sum up the evidence and connect it to your own work.
  • Bibliography: Harvard references, not in the word count.

Around 1,500 words, using at least six sources, at least two of them academic or official (such as Ofcom or the Premier League).

Essay brief

Has the internet really changed who gets to tell sports stories?

ABOUT 1,500 WORDS · 4 MEDIA TYPES · 6+ SOURCES

The question: has the internet really changed who gets to tell sports stories, or are the same big organisations still in charge?

Anyone can now record a podcast in their bedroom and put it on Spotify, or post a video on YouTube or TikTok for free. You don't need a TV studio or a newspaper to reach an audience. But the Premier League's 2023 TV rights sale (£6.7 billion) was the biggest sports media deal ever made in the UK, Sky Sports and talkSPORT still dominate broadcasting, and the most listened-to sports podcasts are still made by big media companies. There is no single right answer: the strongest essays argue that both are true at once. It is much easier to make something, but still very hard to reach a big audience.

The four sections

  1. Podcasts. Ofcom (2024) found around 11.7 million UK adults listen to at least one podcast a week, but the biggest sports podcasts still come from Sky Sports, talkSPORT and The Athletic. Compare one of these with a small independent show such as Non League Heroes, started by a fan on cheap kit and covering clubs the BBC never reports on. Has it become easier to make a podcast, but not easier to get millions to listen?
  2. Video (YouTube and TikTok). COPA90 began in 2012 as a fan-run YouTube channel covering stories Sky ignored. It succeeded, then big companies bought in: Liberty Global took a £7 million stake in 2015 and Time Warner followed in 2017. Do big companies simply buy successful independent voices? And are athletes' own TikTok and YouTube clips genuinely new sports media, or PR run by the same agents and sponsors?
  3. Written journalism. The Athletic charges £9.99 a month and carries no advertisers, so it can write what it likes, but not everyone can pay. Tabloid sport (the Sun, the Mirror, the Daily Mail) is free online. Has this created a two-tier system where the best journalism costs money and everyone else gets clickbait? You could also look at athletes writing Substack newsletters straight to fans, bypassing journalists.
  4. Social media. In June 2020 Marcus Rashford's open letter on Twitter about free school meals led the government to reverse its decision within 48 hours. In May 2021 Naomi Osaka announced her French Open withdrawal on her own accounts, with no press conference. Is this real change, or a new gatekeeping where you need millions of followers and the algorithm on your side?

Your argument

Take a clear position at the start and stick to it. Don't just describe what happened: use the examples as evidence. For example: yes, it is easier to make media, but reaching a mass audience is still controlled by big organisations and algorithms. Or argue the opposite. Either is fine if the evidence backs it up.

Connect it to your own work (where the highest marks are)

  • Why were you able to make your podcast, documentary or video essay? What made it possible?
  • Could your work ever reach as big an audience as a BBC podcast or a Sky Sports channel?
  • What does your honest answer tell you about whether democratisation has really worked?

Classroom tasks · Year 12 and Year 13

Additional tasks

Short editing tasks you can do in one or two lessons using match footage you've been given. Each one builds a skill you'll use in the bigger briefs, and each one goes in your weekly log.

Classroom task

One goal, five angles

1 LESSON · SPEED RAMPS · TELESTRATOR

Take one goal from the match footage, including the broadcast replays, and build a 20-second "every angle" sequence like the ones Sky and the club channels post straight after a goal.

  1. Log the angles. Scrub through the replays and note the timecode of every angle of the goal: main camera, reverse, behind the goal, close-up on the scorer, celebration. You need at least 5.
  2. Build the sequence. Start with the goal at full speed, then cut through the angles. Keep it to 20 seconds.
  3. Speed ramps. On at least two angles, ramp from normal speed into slow motion at the key moment (the strike or the save), then ramp back up.
  4. Freeze-frame and telestrator. Freeze one frame (for example the moment of the pass or the gap in the defence) and draw a circle or arrow on it, like a pundit's telestrator. Hold for 1–2 seconds, then release back into play.
  5. Sound. Keep the crowd and commentary under the angles. Add a whoosh or impact on the freeze if it helps.

Hand in

  • Your 20-second MP4 (16:9 or 9:16)
  • Your angle log with timecodes
  • A timeline screenshot in your weekly log showing the speed ramps and freeze

Software tips

CapCut: Speed → Curve for ramps, Freeze for the still frame, and a circle shape or sticker with a keyframed scale for the telestrator. Premiere Pro: Time Remapping on the clip's speed line for ramps, Frame Hold for the freeze, and the Essential Graphics ellipse for the circle.

What good looks like

Smooth ramps with no jerky jumps, every angle adding something new, a telestrator that points out something the viewer wouldn't have noticed, and the whole thing landing at 20 seconds.

Classroom task

The 10-second hook

1 LESSON · SOCIAL VIDEO · CLASS VOTE

Most people decide whether to keep watching in the first 2 seconds. Using the same match footage as everyone else, make only the first 10 seconds of a social clip, 9:16. The class then votes on which hook would stop them scrolling.

  1. Pick your hook type. For example: start on the goal itself, a big reaction or celebration, a shocking moment (a red card or a miss), a bold caption question ("Was this the goal of the season?"), or a sound-led opening (the commentator's call).
  2. Edit 10 seconds. 9:16, reframed so the action is in shot, with a caption in the first second and the sound you want.
  3. Export as HOOK_Surname.mp4 and add it to the class folder.
  4. Watch and vote. All the hooks are played back to back. Everyone votes for the two that would make them stop scrolling (not their own).
  5. Discuss why. What did the winners do in the first 2 seconds? Movement, faces, text, sound, surprise?

Hand in

  • Your 10-second MP4
  • In your weekly log: a screenshot of your first frame, which hook type you chose, how many votes it got, and one thing you'd change after seeing the winners

Think about

  • What is on screen in the very first frame?
  • Can it be understood with the sound off?
  • Does the caption make you want to know what happens next?

What good looks like

Something happens in the first second, the caption is short and readable on a phone, and the clip makes you want to watch the rest. Follow it up by finishing the clip in the Crystal Palace 1–3 Liverpool brief.

Classroom task

Big game trailer

1–2 LESSONS · MUSIC BUILD · END BOARD

Use past footage of two clubs to build a 30-second "this weekend" preview for a big upcoming game, the kind broadcasters run in the days before kick-off.

  1. Pick the fixture and the story. A derby, a title decider, a cup tie or a revenge match. Write one line that sums up why it matters.
  2. Gather clips. Past goals, celebrations, managers, crowds and key players from both clubs. Log what you use.
  3. Music build. Choose a track that builds. Cut slowly at the start and faster as the music rises, landing your biggest moment on the drop or peak.
  4. Captions. 3–4 short captions that tell the story (for example "Last time…", "Top v second", "Everything to play for").
  5. End board. Finish on the two club names or crests, the date and kick-off time, and the channel it's on, held for at least 3 seconds.

Hand in

  • Your 30-second MP4 (16:9, or 9:16 for social)
  • Your clip log and music choice
  • A timeline screenshot in your weekly log showing your cuts on the beat

Think about

  • Does the edit speed up as the music builds?
  • Would someone who doesn't follow either club understand why the game matters?
  • Is the end board readable and on screen long enough?

What good looks like

Cuts that hit the beat, a clear story in four captions, a strong peak moment, and an end board that looks like a real broadcaster promo.

Classroom task

Deconstruct a pro package

1–2 LESSONS · ANALYSIS → EDIT

Take a professional Sky Sports or BBC highlights or feature package, pull it apart shot by shot, then copy its structure with your own footage.

  1. Choose a package of 1–3 minutes from Sky Sports, BBC Sport or a club channel.
  2. Time every shot. Make a table: start time, length, what the shot shows, how it gets to the next shot (cut, dissolve, wipe, whip) and what's on the soundtrack.
  3. Log the graphics. Every caption, name strap, score bug and logo: when it comes on, how long it stays, how it animates.
  4. Draw it as a timeline. One row for video, one for graphics, one for sound. Mark where the big moments land.
  5. Copy the structure. Using footage you have, rebuild the package with the same rhythm: same number of shots, same shot lengths, same transition types and graphic timings.

Hand in

  • Your shot table and drawn timeline
  • Your copy-the-structure edit as an MP4
  • Both side by side in your weekly log, with 3–4 sentences on what you learned about pace and structure

Think about

  • What's the average shot length? Is it faster at the start or the end?
  • Where does the music change, and what happens on screen when it does?
  • How long are graphics on screen, and why that long?

What good looks like

An accurate, detailed breakdown, and an edit that clearly follows the pro package's rhythm, with a thoughtful reflection on why professionals make those choices.

    Supporting your young person on Sports Media

    Sports Media is a practical, coursework-based course. There are no exams: your young person builds a portfolio of real productions across two years and is graded on it. Here is how it works and how you can help.

    • Encourage them to run the log checker before they publish each week.
    • Watch and listen to what they make. Feedback from a real audience feeds straight into their evaluation.
    • Look out for sports events, clubs and local media they could film, interview or report on.

    The working week

    Year 12 Sports Media runs for 12 hours a week as three 4-hour practical sessions, using the school's 8 radio studios, 3 sound booths and edit suite. Most work is done in those sessions; the weekly log and some research happen alongside.
